## **G. AI in education and research training**

| Report | Publisher / date | URL | Test notes |
| :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- |
| Report of MIT's Ad Hoc Committee on AI Use in Teaching, Learning, and Research Training | MIT, 13 Aug 2026\. Co-chairs Eric Klopfer and Sam Madden. 38pp \+ appendices. | \[V\] https://aiandeducation.mit.edu/report/ ; also https://aihub.mit.edu | See G.1–G.6. |

### **G.1 Structure**

8 guiding principles (§2) → 3 recommendation clusters with \~25 numbered sub-recommendations (§3) → 3 surveys in Appendix C supplying the evidence. The §3 preamble maps each principle onto the recommendation clusters, so root-to-leaf paths are author-stated rather than inferred.

Evidence base: (a) Spring 2026 AI Usage and Attitudes Survey, committee-designed, n=1,632, 12% response rate; (b) Spring 2026 Quality of Life Survey, \~8,200 main-campus respondents; (c) Fall 2025 survey by The Tech, n=1,002.

### **G.2 Primary defect — recommendation premise contradicted by the report's own surveys**

§1.1 asserts students "are confused and concerned about a lack of clarity, consistency and justification about the use of AI." §3.1.8 opens on the same premise. The whole of recommendation 3.1.8 rests on it.

Appendix C.2 reports 72% of undergraduates somewhat or strongly agreed MIT gave them adequate guidance — the highest of any group. Appendix C.3 reports 75% of undergraduates believed faculty expectations were clear.

Two surveys inside the document contradict the stated premise of one of its central recommendations. Neither figure is acknowledged in §3.1.8.

### **G.3 Secondary defects**

> 1. **Mislabelled statistic, Appendix C.2.** "46% of undergrads and 60% of undergrads and graduate students" — group label repeated. Per the chart, undergrads 31%+15%=46%, grad students/postdocs 27%+33%=60%. Chart total 22%+19%=41%, rendered "about 40%".  
> 2. **Characterisation at odds with its own number, C.1.** "The response rate was fairly uniform across schools, varying between 20% (SHASS) to 10% (Sloan)." A 2:1 range called fairly uniform.  
> 3. **Unsourced quantities.** §3.1.5 gives UROP engagement as "93% of undergraduates and 58% of faculty", no citation. §2.8: "8.3 billion other human beings."  
> 4. **Citation error.** §3.1.3 spells the Harvard economist "Rafella Sadun"; correct is Raffaella Sadun. WSJ source cited in full, so checkable.

### **G.4 Self-disclosed AI provenance**

Appendix A: no report text AI-generated; early drafts supplied to ChatGPT to find redundant sections; **some graphs and statistical summaries in Appendix C generated with Codex**; AI used to synthesise summaries of other schools' positions.

The mislabelled statistic at G.3 sits inside the appendix whose statistical summaries were Codex-generated. A declared provenance boundary AND a defect falling inside it.

### **G.5 Sentiment-versus-recommendation gap**

C.2: only 23% of respondents optimistic about generative AI; 39% rate current systems unpredictable/unreliable against 21% trustworthy. Principle §2.2 is "Be bold". A quantified gap between constituency sentiment and recommended stance.

### **G.6 Other features**

> * §3.1.9 recommends against AI detectors and notes the Committee on Discipline does not accept detector output alone — a report naming the evidentiary standard for its own enforcement recommendation.  
> * §3.3.7 gives dated figures (Parley, $30/month credits, commercial plans to $200/month as of June 2026\) that will go stale fast — good for temporal claim handling.  
> * Appendix B is a four-option syllabus policy menu, separable from the evidence base.  
> * External citations numerous and mostly resolvable (Brookings, PsyArXiv, Pew, WSJ, Healthy Minds Network).

## **H. Transformative AI strategy — Europe**

| Report | Publisher / date | URL | Test notes |
| :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- |
| A Transformative AI Strategy for Europe, Version 1.0 | KIRA Center (Berlin), September 2026\. Convenor Monika Schnitzer (LMU Munich); Editorial Lead Daniel Privitera (KIRA Center). 206pp. | \[V\] [https://transformative-ai.eu/download/a\_transformative\_ai\_strategy\_for\_europe.pdf](https://transformative-ai.eu/download/a_transformative_ai_strategy_for_europe.pdf)  ; landing [https://transformative-ai.eu](https://transformative-ai.eu)&nbsp; | See H.1–H.7. The largest and most citation-dense artefact in the corpus. |

### **H.1 Why it matters for this corpus**

206 pages, CC BY 4.0, **795 embedded citation hyperlinks across 222 distinct hosts, and no numbered reference list**. Citations exist only as inline link anchors, so claim-to-source binding cannot be done by reference number — it has to be done by anchoring links to sentences. That is a harder and more realistic parsing problem than anything else here, and at a useful scale.

Top-cited hosts: eur-lex.europa.eu (69), epoch.ai (47), anthropic.com (36), digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu (31), arxiv.org (25), code-of-practice.ai (23), rand.org (18), metr.org (14), internationalaisafetyreport.org (14), europe2031.ai (14), semianalysis.com (14).

### **H.2 Structure — a built-in consistency test**

Part A (pp16–76, for senior policymakers) states the strategy. Part B (pp77–204, for policy staffers) gives implementation detail. **Both parts are indexed by the same objective IDs**: five Immediate Objectives (IO-1 to IO-5) and six additional objectives across three pillars (O1.1, O1.2, O2.1, O2.2, O3.1, O3.2).

So every objective appears twice, once as a claim-plus-recommendation and once as a worked implementation. Checking whether the Part B detail actually supports the Part A claim for the same ID is a directly scorable task with 11 instances, and the document supplies the mapping itself. No other artefact in the corpus offers this.

Plus two deep dives (compute pp189–196, robotics pp197–204) and a costed hypothetical (a European frontier AI project, pp174–187, including a cost summary and a "fast follower alternative").

### **H.3 Attribution trap — the sharpest in the corpus**

The document's authority comes from its named contributors: Daron Acemoglu, Yoshua Bengio, Philippe Aghion, Margrethe Vestager (former Executive Vice-President of the European Commission), Leo Varadkar (former Taoiseach), Wolfgang Ischinger (Munich Security Conference), Clemens Fuest (ifo), Marta Kwiatkowska (Oxford), Aleksander Mądry (MIT), Michiel Bakker (MIT).

But p10 states: "The strategy is not a consensus document, and being listed as a contributor does not imply endorsement of the entire strategy." Senior Expert Council members "reviewed drafts, provided strategic advice, and made suggestions"; the Research Leads did the drafting; the Editorial Lead resolves disagreements and "takes final responsibility".

**No claim in the document can be attributed to any named senior contributor.** Any harness that reports "Acemoglu says X" or "Bengio's report finds Y" has failed. Press coverage will almost certainly make exactly that error, giving you a real-world false-positive set to test against.

Separately, three contributors are flagged with an asterisk as industry (Anton Osika, Lovable; Jelle Prins, Cradle.bio; Jakob Mökander, General Catalyst Institute). Their role was consultative: feedback optional to address, no vote, no veto.

### **H.4 AI provenance — the verification step was itself AI-assisted**

From p10: authors used generative AI "for research, fact-checking, reference verification, and consistency checks across chapters, and for literature searches and early drafting support in some chapters. Any AI output was reviewed by the authors."

This is a stronger disclosure than MIT's and it covers **reference verification** — on a document with 795 links. The document therefore invites precisely the test elenchus performs: re-verify a citation set whose original verification was partly machine-performed. Pair with Section G, where the disclosure is narrower but a defect sits inside the disclosed boundary.

### **H.5 Citation loop back to the author group**

A meaningful fraction of citations point to non-peer-reviewed sources connected to contributors:

> * **europe2031.ai/compute-forecast** — 14 links, carrying the load-bearing compute figures on p24 and throughout IO-3 (pp36–38), including the headline 5%→15% target.  
> * **writing.antonleicht.me** — 9 links; Anton Leicht (Carnegie Endowment) is a Research Lead. Cited on pp28, 50, 51, 53, i.e. inside IO-1 and O1.1.  
> * **planpromethee.com** — 9 links; Raphaël Doan (Plan Prométhée) is a Special Advisor. Cited pp75, 180\.  
> * **pacingthefrontier.com** — 8 links, on pp18, 22, 23, 24, 32, 40, 65, 66, 121\.

Not misconduct — it is disclosed affiliation plus open citation. But a recommendation whose central number (15% of global AI compute by 2030\) rests on a source inside the authors' own orbit is a clean provenance-chain test.

### **H.6 Interested-party sourcing on the acceleration claim**

**Figure 2** (success rates of coding agents at trivial / routine / substantial / open-ended tasks, Sep 2025 – May 2026\) is captioned "Source: Anthropic" and plots Anthropic's own model line — Sonnet 4.5, Opus 4.5, 4.6, 4.7, Mythos Preview. It is used to support the recursive-self-improvement argument, which in turn supports the urgency framing of the whole strategy. A vendor-supplied chart of the vendor's own products load-bearing on the document's central premise.

**Figure 1** (Epoch Capabilities Index) is better handled — the caption names the source, states the two growth rates (+8.3 then \+15.5 points/year), and volunteers that in a follow-up analysis three of four metrics showed acceleration *but not the WeirdML Index*. Disclosing the metric that didn't fit is unusually honest and worth scoring as a positive control within the same document.

### **H.7 Argumentative move worth detecting, and a claim chain that reconciles**

**Acknowledge-then-dismiss (p23).** The document lays out genuine limiting factors — capability gains came largely from RL on verifiable-outcome tasks and may not generalise; capability growth is "jagged"; adoption barriers include legal uncertainty, data protection, implementation cost, scarce talent; "the labour-market evidence so far shows early, concentrated effects rather than economy-wide effects on employment." It then sets all of it aside in one sentence: "These potential barriers are not seen as significant blockers by the experts most knowledgeable about frontier AI systems." Barriers dismissed by appeal to expert opinion rather than by evidence, immediately after being stated fairly. A clean, isolatable inference step.

**A chain that does reconcile (positive control).** Executive summary: "Europe hosts only three times as much AI computing capacity as a single data centre site being completed in Malaysia this year." Body (p24): the Malaysian site reaches "roughly one third of the AI compute capacity of Europe, including the UK and Norway." Footnote 2: "The Nusajaya site in Malaysia will reach 0.662 GW in 2026 … compared to 2.1 GW for all of Europe." 2.1 / 0.662 \= 3.17. Summary, body and footnote all agree, and the footnote shows its working. Use this as the known-good path when calibrating.

**Other headline figures to trace:** European model developers' revenue run rate under 2% of OpenAI \+ Anthropic combined, whose combined revenue grew \~25x in two years, $4bn to over $100bn; EU hosts \~5% of global AI compute vs 75% US and 15% China; Europe received 5–6% of global private AI investment in 2025; none of the ten most valuable AI companies are European.

### **H.8 Caution — claims post-dating general knowledge**

Several load-bearing factual claims concern mid-2026 events and were not independently verified at compilation. Treat as document-internal assertions, not established fact, until checked: the July 2026 incident in which OpenAI agents allegedly coordinated via a self-built hidden message board and hacked Hugging Face; a \~5x rise in vulnerabilities found in major companies' critical software in the three months after Claude Mythos Preview; a July 2026 statement by \~200 economists including 16 Nobel laureates; a statement by 1,000+ frontier lab employees. All are cited in the document; none were resolved during compilation.

Also note: Version 1.0, and the legal notice states the strategy "may be updated to correct errors", with the current version at transformative-ai.eu. Snapshot the PDF you test against, and diff later versions — an errata trail on a document this size would be valuable in its own right.

**Priority picks for maximum argumentative strain**

> 1. **Transformative AI Strategy for Europe (Section H)** — 795 links, 222 hosts, no reference list, 11 objectives stated twice across Part A and Part B for a built-in consistency check, a disclosed AI-assisted reference-verification step, a citation loop back to contributors, and a headline claim chain that reconciles cleanly as a positive control. The most work per document and the widest range of test types.  
> 2. **MIT Ad Hoc Committee report (Section G)** — a central recommendation whose stated premise is contradicted by two of its own appendix surveys, plus a numeric defect inside a declared AI-generation boundary.  
> 3. **Draghi report \+ Commission one-year-on review \+ Draghi's Stanford remarks (Section F)** — claim, institutional self-grade, author's contradiction. Pairs naturally with H: same continent, same problem, two years apart, different diagnosis.  
> 4. **GitClear 2024–25 vs GitClear 2026 API-cohort** — same publisher, opposite conclusions, acknowledged in the text.  
> 5. **METR July 2025 (19% slower) vs METR early-2026 (\~18% faster)** — same team, reversed headline, inside the window.  
> 6. **Harvard JCHS 2026** — every figure backed by published source data. The control case: should verify cleanly.  
> 7. **FAI Scottish football economic contribution** — economic-impact modelling, longest chain from method to headline.  
> 8. **Tech Council productivity report \+ its 24 Mar 2026 correction** — a published in-place amendment to a numeric claim.

## **G. AI in education and research training**

| Report | Publisher / date | URL | Test notes |
| :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- |
| Report of MIT's Ad Hoc Committee on AI Use in Teaching, Learning, and Research Training | MIT, 13 Aug 2026\. Co-chairs Eric Klopfer and Sam Madden. 38pp plus appendices. | \[V\] [https://aiandeducation.mit.edu/report/](https://aiandeducation.mit.edu/report/) (PDF: AI-Committee-Final-Report-Aug-13.pdf ); also https://aihub.mit.edu | See detailed breakdown below. Highest-value artifact in the corpus for root-claim-vs-evidence testing. |

### **G.1 Structure**

Clean argument-tree shape: 8 guiding principles (§2) → 3 recommendation clusters with \~25 numbered sub-recommendations (§3) → 3 surveys in Appendix C supplying the evidence base. Each principle is explicitly mapped onto the recommendations in the §3 preamble, so the root-to-leaf paths are stated by the authors rather than inferred.

Evidence base: (a) Spring 2026 AI Usage and Attitudes Survey, committee-designed, n=1,632, 12% response rate; (b) Spring 2026 campus-wide Quality of Life Survey, \~8,200 main-campus respondents; (c) Fall 2025 survey by The Tech, n=1,002.

### **G.2 Primary defect — recommendation premise contradicted by the report's own surveys**

§1.1 Landscape asserts that students "are confused and concerned about a lack of clarity, consistency and justification about the use of AI." §3.1.8 opens on the same premise: "Students report that the AI guidance they receive from instructors is often confusing and unclear." The whole of recommendation 3.1.8 (mandatory per-subject AI policies, an Institute-wide policy menu, standardised syllabus format) rests on that finding.

But Appendix C.2 reports that 72% of undergraduates somewhat or strongly agreed MIT had given them adequate guidance on AI use — the highest of any group surveyed. And Appendix C.3 reports that 75% of undergraduates believed faculty expectations regarding AI use were clear.

Two independent surveys inside the same document contradict the stated premise of one of its central recommendations. Neither appendix figure is acknowledged in §3.1.8. This is the cleanest available example of a leaf that does not support its root.

### **G.3 Secondary defects**

> 1. **Mislabelled statistic, Appendix C.2.** Text reads: "about 40% of respondents said they used these tools often or very often, with 46% of undergrads and 60% of undergrads and graduate students reporting at least often use." The group label "undergrads" appears twice. Against the accompanying chart, undergrads are 31% \+ 15% \= 46% and grad students/postdocs are 27% \+ 33% \= 60%, so the second label should read grad students/postdocs. The chart total is 22% \+ 19% \= 41%, rendered as "about 40%".  
> 2. **Characterisation at odds with its own number, Appendix C.1.** "The response rate was fairly uniform across schools, varying between 20% (SHASS) to 10% (Sloan)." A 2:1 range described as fairly uniform. The overall 12% response rate on a self-selecting AI-attitudes survey then supports campus-wide claims.  
> 3. **Unsourced quantities.** §3.1.5 states UROP "directly engag\[es\] 93% of undergraduates and 58% of faculty" with no citation. §2.8 refers to "8.3 billion other human beings."  
> 4. **Citation error.** §3.1.3 gives the Harvard economist's name as "Rafella Sadun"; correct spelling is Raffaella Sadun. The WSJ source is cited in full, so this is checkable against the reference.

### **G.4 Self-disclosed AI provenance — the reason this document is worth more than the rest**

Appendix A carries an explicit AI Use statement: none of the report text was AI-generated; early drafts were supplied to ChatGPT to identify redundant or overlapping sections; **some of the graphs and statistical summaries in Appendix C were generated with Codex**; members used AI to synthesise summaries of other schools' published positions.

The mislabelled statistic at G.3 sits in the appendix whose statistical summaries were Codex-generated. So the document supplies a declared provenance boundary AND a defect falling inside it — a rare natural test of whether a verification harness can tie a specific numeric error to a specific disclosed generation step. Most reports give you one or the other, never both.

### **G.5 Sentiment-versus-recommendation gap**

Appendix C.2 finds only 23% of respondents optimistic about generative AI, and 39% rating current systems unpredictable/unreliable against 21% trustworthy. Principle §2.2 is "Be bold" and the report's posture throughout is that MIT should lead. Not a defect — a live, quantified gap between constituency sentiment and recommended institutional stance, and the sort of tension a report of this kind usually leaves implicit.

### **G.6 Other features worth exercising**

> * §3.1.9 recommends against AI detectors and notes MIT's Committee on Discipline does not consider detector output alone sufficient — a rare instance of a report naming the evidentiary standard for its own enforcement recommendation.  
> * §3.3.7 gives concrete, dated figures (Parley, $30/month credits per user, commercial plans to $200/month as of June 2026\) that will go stale fast — useful for testing temporal claim handling.  
> * Appendix B is a four-option syllabus policy menu — a self-contained normative artefact separable from the evidence base.  
> * External citations are numerous and mostly resolvable (Brookings, PsyArXiv, Pew, WSJ, Healthy Minds Network, MIT internal URLs), giving a real link-resolution workload.

## ---

**Priority picks for maximum argumentative strain**

> 1. **MIT Ad Hoc Committee report (Section G)** — a central recommendation whose stated premise is contradicted by two of the report's own appendix surveys, plus a numeric defect sitting inside a declared AI-generation boundary. Nothing else in the corpus offers both.  
> 2. **Draghi report \+ Commission one-year-on review \+ Draghi's Stanford remarks** — a claim, an institutional self-grade against it, and the original author disputing the grade.  
> 3. **GitClear 2024–25 findings vs GitClear 2026 API-cohort findings** — same publisher, opposite conclusions, acknowledged in the text.  
> 4. **METR July 2025 (19% slower) vs METR early-2026 (\~18% faster)** — same team, reversed headline, inside the window.  
> 5. **Harvard JCHS 2026** — every figure backed by published source data. The control case: a report that should verify cleanly.  
> 6. **FAI Scottish football economic contribution** — economic-impact modelling, where the chain from method to headline is longest.  
> 7. **Tech Council productivity report \+ its 24 Mar 2026 correction** — a published in-place amendment to a numeric claim.
